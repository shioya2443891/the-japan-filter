## Two Knives, Same Steel, Different Job

Kaizen II's santoku and gyuto share identical construction -- FC61 fine-carbide steel core, Damascus cladding, 60-62 HRC hardness, and the same Honbazuke hand-finished edge, per manufacturer specifications. Neither shape is the "upgraded" version of the other. The choice comes down to blade geometry, and each shape traces back to a different moment in Japanese knife history.

## Santoku: A Post-War Home-Cook Invention

Santoku developed in Japan after World War II as a home-cook alternative that folded three more specialized traditional blades -- the deba for fish, the nakiri for vegetables, and the gyuto for meat -- into a single general-purpose knife, per the cutlery-history sources reviewed. The name reflects that design goal directly: san (three) + toku (virtues). It kept the nakiri's flat profile and added a rounded "sheep's foot" tip, producing a blade meant to do most home-kitchen cutting tasks reasonably well rather than one task exceptionally well.

## Gyuto: A Meiji-Era Reinterpretation of the Western Knife

Gyuto has a much older and different lineage. It emerged during the Meiji era, after the 1876 Haitorei edict ended the right to carry swords and pushed swordsmiths in Japan's traditional blade-making centers to pivot toward kitchen cutlery. As the Meiji government promoted a meat-inclusive diet -- a break from centuries of restricted meat consumption -- cooks needed a knife suited to breaking down larger cuts of beef and preparing Western-style dishes. Japanese blacksmiths adapted the Western chef's knife profile, with its longer blade and curved belly, using Japanese steel and forging technique. The name literally means "beef knife."

## How the Shape Changes the Way You Cut

Santoku's flat edge and sheep's foot tip are built for a push-cut, up-and-down chopping motion -- the blade contacts the cutting board across most of its length with each stroke, which suits dicing vegetables and general prep. Gyuto's curved belly is built for a rocking cut, a motion US home cooks commonly use with a Western chef's knife -- rolling the blade forward through longer, sweeping strokes, which suits slicing and breaking down larger cuts of meat. Neither motion is more "correct"; they're matched to different shapes for a reason rooted in each blade's origin.

## Edge Angle Is the Same Either Way

Whichever shape you choose, both are ground to approximately the same 9.5-12 degree angle and finished using Miyabi's Honbazuke hand-finishing process, per manufacturer specifications. That means sharpening approach doesn't change based on blade shape -- a whetstone matched to that acute edge angle is the right tool for either knife. See our [Miyabi Honbazuke sharpening guide](/knives/miyabi-honbazuke-sharpening-guide/) for what that process involves and how to maintain the edge at home.

## Which One Should You Buy First

If you're buying a single Japanese knife to handle most home cooking -- vegetables, fish, and meat in roughly equal measure -- santoku follows its original design intent most directly. If you already cook with a Western chef's knife regularly, especially for meat-heavy prep, and want to add Japanese steel without changing your cutting technique, gyuto is the more direct transition. Cooks who eventually want both get a genuinely complementary pair rather than a duplicate: the two shapes are suited to different tasks, not different skill levels. Buyers deciding whether to also step up in materials, rather than shape, should see our [Kaizen II vs. Birchwood SG2 comparison](/knives/miyabi-kaizen-vs-birchwood/) for what the price jump between tiers actually buys.

## Bottom Line

Santoku and gyuto cost about the same and share the same steel, hardness, and edge finishing in Miyabi's Kaizen II line -- the difference is entirely in blade geometry and the cutting motion each shape was built for. Match the shape to how you actually cook rather than treating one as the default: santoku for an all-purpose, vegetable-forward home kitchen, gyuto for a more meat-focused, rocking-cut technique carried over from a Western chef's knife.

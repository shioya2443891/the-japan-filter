## A Sword-Finishing Technique, Adapted for Kitchen Knives

Honbazuke shows up in Miyabi's product descriptions without much explanation, but per Zwilling's own production materials, it's a specific three-stage hand-finishing process for the cutting edge, performed by a small number of qualified specialists at the Seki City facility. The manufacturer describes its origins as tracing back to the final-edge techniques used in traditional Japanese swordmaking.

## What It Actually Changes About the Edge

The practical result of Honbazuke, per manufacturer specifications, is a thinner, more acute edge than typical Western double-bevel chef's knives. That's part of why a Miyabi knife feels notably sharp new -- and it's also the reason standard Western sharpening tools aren't necessarily a good match for maintaining it.

## Why Your Regular Sharpener Might Be the Wrong Tool

Pull-through and fixed-angle sharpeners are commonly built around a wider edge angle than Honbazuke produces. Using one on a Miyabi blade can remove more material than necessary over time, or fail to match the edge's actual geometry. A whetstone lets you sharpen at an angle closer to the knife's original edge -- more effort up front, but a better match for the construction.

## No Confirmed Free Sharpening Program

Shun, a comparably priced competitor also manufactured in Seki City, offers a documented free lifetime sharpening program for US buyers -- see our [guide to Shun's free sharpening service](/knives/shun-free-sharpening-service/) for the specific eligibility terms. We did not find evidence of a comparable program for Miyabi within the official materials reviewed. That's not a flaw in the knife itself, but it is a real difference in total cost of ownership worth factoring into a purchase decision between the two brands.

## Bottom Line

Honbazuke is a genuine, verifiable piece of craftsmanship behind Miyabi's edge, not just marketing language -- and it comes with a practical maintenance implication most buyers don't learn about until after the purchase. A whetstone in the 1000-6000 grit range, and the willingness to use it, is what actually preserves the benefit of that hand-finished edge over time.

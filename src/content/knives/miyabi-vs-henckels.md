## One Company, Two Brand Names

The instinct to compare Miyabi and Henckels as rival brands is understandable -- both names are associated with the Zwilling J.A. Henckels company, and both show up in the same "best chef's knife" roundups. But they aren't rivals inside the business. Zwilling J.A. Henckels operates Zwilling (the premium brand, which owns Miyabi) and Henckels (the entry-level brand) as two separate tiers of the same company, per company brand materials. Choosing between Miyabi and Henckels is closer to choosing between a car maker's flagship division and its budget division than choosing between two competitors.

## Where Each Tier Is Actually Made

This is where the split becomes concrete rather than just a marketing distinction. Miyabi is made at a Zwilling-owned factory in Seki City, Japan -- a facility the company built specifically for this line, per company materials. Henckels' manufacturing is spread across multiple countries depending on the collection: the forged Classic line is officially listed as made in Spain on henckels.com, while the stamped Statement collection -- Henckels' best-selling line -- does not publish a country of manufacture on its official product pages. Multiple third-party retail and review sources describe Henckels' stamped collections generally as manufactured in China, Thailand, or India, though we could not confirm this directly from Henckels' own specifications for the Statement line specifically.

The practical takeaway: "Henckels" is not a single country of origin. A buyer who wants to know exactly where a specific Henckels knife was made needs to check that product's page individually, not assume the brand name implies one answer.

## Steel, Hardness, and Construction Differences

Miyabi's Kaizen II line uses FC61 steel hardened to approximately 61 HRC, per manufacturer specifications -- a figure Zwilling publishes directly for the Miyabi line. Henckels Classic is forged from German stainless steel, but henckels.com does not publish a Rockwell hardness figure for the line, and we did not find a manufacturer-confirmed HRC number during our research. That's a meaningful gap in what each brand discloses, not just a difference in the number itself: a buyer comparing edge retention has a documented figure for Miyabi and an undocumented one for Henckels.

Construction also differs by Henckels line. Classic is fully forged, similar in method to Miyabi and to Zwilling's own German-made knives, just with a lower hardness target and a lower price. Statement and most of Henckels' other collections use stamped, single-piece blade construction, which is lighter and less expensive to produce but generally regarded as a different tier of knife-making than forged construction. If you're comparing Henckels to Miyabi on build method, Classic is the fairer comparison -- Statement is a step down again within the Henckels name itself.

## What This Means for a Home Kitchen

A cook on a tighter budget who wants a forged, German-steel knife from a company with over a century of cutlery history gets a reasonable, well-priced option in Henckels Classic. A cook who specifically wants Miyabi's harder, thinner Japanese-style edge -- and who is willing to pay roughly two to three times as much and manage a less forgiving blade -- gets that from the Miyabi tier instead. Miyabi's edge is also hand-finished differently than either Henckels line; see our [Miyabi honbazuke sharpening guide](/knives/miyabi-honbazuke-sharpening-guide/) for what that means for maintenance specifically.

Neither choice is a mistake. The mistake would be assuming that because both names trace back to Zwilling J.A. Henckels, they represent the same quality tier with a different sticker on the box. They don't -- the parent company built them to be different on purpose.

## Bottom Line

Miyabi and Henckels aren't two brands competing for the same customer -- they're two manufacturing tiers the same company runs side by side, one built around a company-owned Japanese factory and a documented hardness spec, the other built around lower-cost, multi-country sourcing and a lower published spec sheet. The better buy depends on how much a buyer values a forged, budget-friendly Western knife versus a harder, Japan-made edge -- not on which name sounds more premium.

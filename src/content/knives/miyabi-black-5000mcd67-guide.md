## A Flagship With a Confusing Price Trail

Miyabi Black sits at the top of the US-marketed lineup, built on MC66 micro-carbide powder steel hardened to 65-66 HRC -- a genuine step up from the mid-tier Kaizen II line, per manufacturer specifications. What complicates a simple "is it worth it" answer is the Japan-market pricing for the closest comparable model, which did not track lower than the US price the way it did for Miyabi's mid tier in the pricing we reviewed.

## What the Steel Actually Buys

MC66 steel's higher hardness translates into an edge that holds up longer between sharpenings than Kaizen II's FC61 steel. The tradeoff is the same one that applies across harder steel formulations generally: less forgiveness around bone, frozen food, or hard cutting surfaces. A buyer who already handles knives carefully gets real value from this; a buyer who wants a knife that tolerates rougher daily use may find the softer Kaizen II steel more practical.

## The Japan Price Doesn't Confirm a Discount

Based on the pricing we reviewed, Japan-market pricing at Miyabi's mid tier ran clearly lower than the equivalent US listing. At the flagship tier, that pattern did not hold in the pricing we reviewed -- the Japan-market listing for a comparable 5000MCD67 santoku priced higher than the US Miyabi Black chef's knife. Blade shape differs between the two listings we compared, so this isn't a precise apples-to-apples figure, but it's enough to say a US buyer should not assume the flagship tier is cheaper to source from Japan.

## Who Should Actually Spend $300+

The flagship premium makes the most sense for cooks who already have experience with premium Japanese steel, sharpen carefully and infrequently, and want the longest possible interval between sharpenings. Cooks newer to premium Japanese knives, or those who want a lower-maintenance daily knife, get most of Miyabi's core advantages from Kaizen II at a meaningfully lower price. For a middle option between Kaizen II and Black, see our [Miyabi Artisan line guide](/knives/miyabi-artisan-line-guide/) -- it uses the same SG2 core steel as Birchwood at a price just below Black. Whichever tier you choose, the edge is hand-finished using the same honbazuke process -- see our [Miyabi honbazuke sharpening guide](/knives/miyabi-honbazuke-sharpening-guide/) for how to maintain it at home without damaging the edge geometry. Buyers weighing this same steel and hardness against a limited-edition design instead of a mainline finish should see our [Miyabi IKI 20th anniversary guide](/knives/miyabi-iki-series-guide/), which uses the identical MC66 steel at a higher price.

## Bottom Line

Miyabi Black's flagship positioning is backed by real, documented material differences -- not just marketing. But the usual assumption that a Japan-made flagship product is cheaper closer to its source didn't hold up in the pricing we reviewed. Buy it through an authorized US channel if the harder edge and higher price are worth it to you; otherwise, Kaizen II covers most of the same ground for less.

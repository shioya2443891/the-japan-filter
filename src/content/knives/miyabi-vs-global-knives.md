## Two Manufacturing Traditions, Not Two Tiers of the Same Thing

Miyabi and Global both show up on "best Japanese chef's knife" roundups, and both sit in a comparable premium price bracket in the US market. That similarity ends once you look at how each knife is actually built. Miyabi is forged and clad -- a hardened steel core wrapped in cladding layers, hand-finished at the edge -- made at a Zwilling J.A. Henckels-owned facility in Seki City. Global is molded from a single piece of stainless steel, blade and handle in one continuous unit, with no forging step at all, made by Yoshikin in Tsubame-Sanjo. Both are genuinely "Made in Japan." They come from two different regional manufacturing traditions that happen to both end up producing kitchen knives.

## Why the Regional History Matters Here

Seki City's cutlery industry descends from centuries of sword production, and the region is commonly grouped with Solingen and Sheffield as one of the world's three historic cutlery centers. Miyabi's forged, clad-steel construction and its hand-finished Honbazuke edge are consistent with that forging lineage. Tsubame-Sanjo's manufacturing base, by contrast, grew out of nail making and then Western-style tableware and flatware production -- Yoshikin itself started in 1954 making hollow-handle Western cutlery for export, decades before it launched the Global knife brand in 1985. Based on Yoshikin's documented company history, Global's hollow, sand-filled handle appears to be a direct outgrowth of that tableware-manufacturing approach, not a scaled-down version of a forged blade.

## Hardness and Maintenance Are the Practical Difference

Miyabi's Kaizen II line uses FC61 steel hardened to roughly 61 HRC, per manufacturer specifications -- noticeably harder than Global G-2's Cromova 18 steel at 56-58 HRC. The harder Miyabi edge holds its sharpness longer between sharpenings but chips more easily and needs a whetstone rather than a pull-through sharpener to maintain properly; our [Miyabi Honbazuke sharpening guide](/knives/miyabi-honbazuke-sharpening-guide/) covers what that involves in practice. Global's softer steel dulls somewhat faster but is more forgiving of careless handling, and its seamless, one-piece construction removes the handle-to-blade crevice that a knife like Miyabi has -- a genuine, if modest, hygiene and cleaning advantage.

## Price Follows the Process

Based on the construction processes described in each manufacturer's official materials, Miyabi's higher price tier ($190 and up) plausibly reflects its more labor-intensive production -- forging, cladding, and hand-finishing steps that Global's one-piece molding process does not require. This is an inference drawn from the manufacturing steps each brand publishes, not a cost breakdown confirmed by either company, but it lines up with Global's core G-Series sitting consistently below Miyabi's core Kaizen tier across the listings reviewed.

## What This Means for a Home Kitchen

A cook who wants an edge that stays sharp through heavy weekly use and is willing to maintain it with a whetstone gets more out of Miyabi's harder steel. A cook who wants one low-maintenance knife that resists corrosion, cleans easily, and doesn't demand much sharpening technique will likely be happier with Global. Neither approach is a compromise version of the other -- they are two different, equally legitimate answers to how a Japanese kitchen knife can be built.

## Bottom Line

Miyabi and Global are not a "better vs. worse" Japanese knife comparison -- they're a forged-and-clad knife from Seki City's swordsmithing tradition against a molded-and-seamless knife from Tsubame-Sanjo's tableware-manufacturing tradition. The right pick depends on how much sharpening maintenance a buyer is willing to take on and how much they're willing to pay for a harder edge.

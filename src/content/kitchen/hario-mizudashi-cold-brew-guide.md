## One Pitcher, Two Traditions

The Hario Mizudashi pot is sold under two separate listings by the same manufacturer -- one marketed for cold brew coffee, one marketed for cold-brewed tea. That's not a case of a coffee product being creatively repurposed by users for tea; both use cases are official, and the underlying method, mizudashi, predates its coffee application as a way of preparing green tea, hojicha, and mugicha in Japan.

## Why "Cold Brew" Means Something Different Here

US cold brew culture is built almost entirely around concentrate: steep coarsely ground coffee at a high ratio for 12 to 24 hours, then dilute the result with water, milk, or ice before drinking. The Mizudashi pot's steeping ratio is calibrated differently -- it's designed to produce coffee or tea at drinking strength directly from the pitcher, without a dilution step. If you buy this expecting to stretch a concentrate across several servings the way you might with a typical US cold brew maker, the ratio and yield won't match your expectations.

## What That Means for How You Use It

Because there's no dilution step built into the intended method, portion planning matters more here than with concentrate systems. The 600ml and 1000ml sizes aren't just about counter or fridge space -- they roughly determine how many drinking-strength servings you get per batch. A single-person household brewing coffee daily may find the 600ml size more practical than a liter that sits partially used in the fridge.

## Worth Buying for the Tea Use Case Alone

If you don't drink cold brew coffee at all but you're curious about cold-brewed green tea or hojicha, the Mizudashi pot is a legitimate way to try both without buying separate equipment -- the pitcher and mesh filter basket are the same across coffee and tea use, according to the manufacturer's own listings. That dual utility is the strongest reason to choose this over a coffee-only cold brew maker, even at a comparable price point.

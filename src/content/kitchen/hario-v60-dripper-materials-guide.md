## The Shape Is the Same -- the Material Isn't

Every Hario V60 dripper shares the same cone angle, the single large hole at the base, and the spiral ribbing that runs up the interior wall. That geometry is the actual brewing design, and it doesn't change across materials. What changes is weight, heat retention, breakage risk, price, and -- for two of the four materials -- a documented production origin in Japan that the other two don't carry in the listings reviewed for this guide.

## Plastic: The Low-Risk Starting Point

The plastic V60 is the least expensive version and the one most first-time buyers encounter. It's also the lightest and the least likely to break if dropped, which makes it a reasonable choice for travel or camping setups. Reviewers commonly describe it as producing coffee indistinguishable from the more expensive materials, since the extraction geometry is identical. Its main tradeoff is heat retention -- plastic loses heat faster during a pour than ceramic or glass, according to manufacturer specifications.

## Glass: Visible Brewing, Documented Origin

The glass version costs roughly double the plastic version and lets you watch the coffee bed and bloom during brewing -- something the ceramic and plastic versions don't offer. It's also the material with the most direct connection to Hario's original business: the company was founded as a scientific glassware maker in 1921, and its proprietary heatproof glass formulation is produced at its own factory in Koga City, Ibaraki Prefecture. It's the most breakage-prone of the four materials, so it's a better fit for a stationary kitchen setup than for travel.

## Ceramic: The Balanced Pick

Ceramic sits in a similar price range to glass but carries the most retained heat of the four materials and holds up better against everyday handling. It's also the material with the most specific documented origin: Hario's official product page states the ceramic V60 is produced in Arita, a porcelain-producing region in Saga Prefecture with a history of more than 400 years. Reviewers commonly describe it as the version they upgrade to after starting with plastic.

## Metal: Durability Without the Weight Tradeoff of Glass

Stainless steel is the most expensive of the four materials in most listings reviewed, and it offers the lowest breakage risk alongside heat retention closer to ceramic than to plastic. It doesn't carry the same documented regional origin claim as the ceramic or glass versions in the sources reviewed for this guide, but it's a reasonable choice for buyers who prioritize durability above all else -- particularly for a dripper that travels between kitchens or gets used outdoors.

## Which One to Actually Buy

If this is your first V60, start with plastic -- it's the lowest financial and physical risk, and it will tell you whether the pour-over method suits your routine before you invest more. If you already know you'll keep using it, ceramic offers the best combination of heat retention, everyday durability, and documented origin. Choose glass specifically if watching the brew matters to you, and metal specifically if breakage risk is your primary concern.

## A Cost That Doesn't Show Up in the Dripper's Price Tag

Most V60 buying advice focuses entirely on the dripper -- material, size, price -- and treats the filters as an afterthought. But filters are a genuine recurring cost, and for a daily coffee drinker, that ongoing expense adds up over the life of the dripper in a way the one-time purchase price doesn't reflect.

## Filter Size, Not Material, Is What Matters

The detail most first-time buyers miss: V60 filters are sized to match the dripper size number (01, 02, etc.), not the dripper material. A size 02 filter fits the plastic, glass, ceramic, and metal size 02 drippers interchangeably. If you're building out a V60 collection across multiple materials, you don't need a separate filter type for each -- one filter size covers all of them at that size. The same size rule carries over to the 2025 V60 NEO -- if you are deciding between the original V60 and the NEO, filter compatibility is not a differentiating factor. See [our comparison of the two versions](/kitchen/hario-v60-neo-vs-original/) for what is.

## Bleached vs. Unbleached: Mostly a Preference Call

Hario sells both bleached (white) and unbleached (natural/brown) filters. Functionally, they're close to equivalent -- some reviewers report a slightly more noticeable papery taste from unbleached filters on the first pour, but rinsing the filter with hot water before brewing is a commonly cited step that reduces this regardless of which type you use. Neither is a clear upgrade over the other; it's largely a personal preference decision.

## The Filterless Alternative

If the ongoing cost and restocking hassle of paper filters bothers you, Hario's own metal V60 dripper uses a built-in stainless steel mesh instead of paper. It costs more upfront than the plastic or ceramic versions, but it eliminates the recurring filter purchase entirely -- you rinse and reuse the mesh rather than buying more filters. The tradeoff is cup character: mesh doesn't trap fine coffee particles and oils the way paper does, so the resulting brew is less clarified and has more body and sediment than a paper-filtered cup. That's not necessarily worse, but it is different, and it's worth deciding based on taste preference rather than assuming the metal version is a strict upgrade.

## What to Actually Buy

For most buyers, sticking with genuine Hario paper filters sized to your dripper is the simplest and lowest-risk choice -- they're confirmed available on Amazon.com, and the size-matching rule means restocking is straightforward even across multiple dripper materials. If you brew multiple cups daily and the ongoing filter cost has become a real consideration, the metal V60 dripper is worth the higher upfront cost, provided you're comfortable with the different cup character it produces.
